Nucleophilic substitution

Rolling wiki generated from your indexed sources · 8 linked concepts

Overview

Nucleophilic substitution is a class of reactions in which an electron-rich nucleophile bonds to an electrophilic carbon atom, displacing a leaving group. It is one of the most important reaction families in organic chemistry because it interconverts functional groups under mild conditions.

Two limiting mechanisms describe how the bond-making and bond-breaking steps are timed. In the SN2 pathway both events happen in a single concerted step, so the rate depends on both the substrate and the nucleophile. In the SN1 pathway the leaving group departs first to form a carbocation, and the rate depends only on the substrate.

Key intuition

Picture an umbrella flipping inside-out in a gust of wind — that is the stereochemical inversion (Walden inversion) the carbon centre undergoes during an SN2 backside attack.

What controls the rate

  • Substrate structure — primary substrates favour SN2; tertiary favour SN1 via a stabilised carbocation.
  • Nucleophile strength — strong, unhindered nucleophiles accelerate SN2.
  • Leaving-group ability — weaker bases (I⁻, TsO⁻) leave more readily.
  • Solvent — polar aprotic solvents leave the nucleophile "naked" and reactive, boosting SN2.

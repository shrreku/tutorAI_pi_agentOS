CONCEPT · ORGANIC CHEMISTRY I

SN2 mechanism

A one-step, bimolecular nucleophilic substitution: the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group, inverting the carbon's configuration.
Ready to study Stereochemistry · 45% · needs review Ch. 7 · p. 142

Definition

The SN2 reaction (Substitution, Nucleophilic, Bimolecular) is a concerted process in which a nucleophile forms a bond to the electrophilic carbon at the same moment the leaving group departs. Because both the substrate and the nucleophile appear in the rate-determining step, the reaction is second order overall: rate = k[substrate][nucleophile].

There is no carbocation intermediate. Bond-making and bond-breaking happen in a single transition state, which gives the SN2 mechanism its hallmark stereochemical signature.

INTUITION

Picture an umbrella flipping inside-out in a gust of wind. As the nucleophile pushes in from the back, the three remaining groups on the carbon swing through to the other side — the stereocentre inverts. Chemists call this Walden inversion.

Key factors

Substrate structure · Primary substrates react fastest; steric crowding at the carbon raises the transition-state energy, so tertiary substrates barely react via SN2.
Nucleophile strength · Strong, charged nucleophiles (e.g. CN⁻, OH⁻) accelerate the bimolecular step.
Solvent · Polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, acetone) leave the nucleophile relatively "naked" and reactive.
Leaving group · Weak bases such as I⁻ and tosylate stabilise the departing charge and speed the reaction.
COMPARE WITH SN1

SN1 runs through a planar carbocation, giving first-order kinetics and a racemic mixture — the opposite of SN2's concerted, stereospecific inversion. Seeing them side by side is the fastest way to lock in when each one dominates.

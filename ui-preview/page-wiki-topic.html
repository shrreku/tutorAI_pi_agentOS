Nucleophilic substitution

The family of reactions in which a nucleophile replaces a leaving group on an electrophilic carbon — the foundation for Chapter 7 of Organic Chemistry I.

Overview

Nucleophilic substitution describes a broad class of organic reactions in which an electron-rich species — the nucleophile — donates a pair of electrons to an electrophilic carbon, displacing another group, the leaving group. It is one of the most common transformations in organic chemistry and underpins everything from laboratory synthesis to the biochemistry of DNA methylation.

Although dozens of variations exist, two limiting mechanisms anchor the topic: SN1 and SN2. They sit at opposite ends of a continuum and differ in molecularity, stereochemical outcome, rate law, and the conditions that favour them. Most real reactions fall somewhere between these extremes, but understanding the two limits lets you predict products and rates with confidence.

RULE OF THUMB

Crowded carbon and a stabilised cation → SN1. Open, primary carbon and a strong nucleophile → SN2. When in doubt, look at the substrate first: sterics usually decide the contest.

The SN2 pathway

In an SN2 reaction the nucleophile approaches from the side directly opposite the leaving group (a backside attack). At the transition state the carbon is briefly bonded to five groups, with the incoming and outgoing groups arranged linearly. As the leaving group departs, the other three substituents flip through to the far side, inverting the stereocentre. Because both partners are present in the rate-determining step, the reaction is bimolecular and sensitive to steric bulk.

The SN1 pathway

SN1 begins with slow, reversible ionisation of the substrate to form a planar carbocation, followed by rapid attack of the nucleophile. Because the flat cation can be attacked from either face, the product is typically a racemic mixture. Rate depends only on substrate concentration, and the reaction is accelerated by stable carbocations and polar protic solvents that solvate the developing charges.
